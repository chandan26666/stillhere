Some patients who seem unresponsive might still be aware.

The usual bedside checks ask a patient to move. We wanted to see if a laptop screen and a 4-sensor EEG headset could check whether someone follows a simple instruction without moving at all. If the signal isn't good enough, it doesn't give a score.

The problem

Aware inside, unable to show it.

After a stroke, a brain injury or a cardiac arrest, some patients have their eyes open but don't respond to anything. Bedside tests need some kind of movement, so if someone is aware but can't move, they look the same as someone who isn't.

How it works

How a test runs

When you look at something flickering, the visual part of your brain (at the back of the head) picks up the same rhythm. If you pay attention to one side, that side's rhythm gets a bit stronger, even if your eyes don't move. That's what we measure.

01

1. Check the signal

Both halves flicker (8.57 Hz and 12 Hz). We need to see both of them in the EEG. If we can't, the test stops there and no score is shown.

02

2. Give commands

A voice says "focus on the left side" or "right side", 12 times in random order (6 each). Eyes stay on the dot. The EEG is used to guess which side they focused on.

03

3. Score it

They need 10 out of 12 to pass. One pass just means "test again later". It only suggests a referral after two passes in a row, at least 12 hours apart.

Eyes closed? There's a second test.

With eyes closed, the back of the brain shows strong alpha waves, and doing mental math usually makes them drop. So the voice says "count backwards from 600 by sevens" or "just relax", 12 times in random order, and we check whether the alpha follows. Same rules and pass mark. In some people alpha goes up during math instead, so they'd get "No conclusion".

Rules

Rules we added so one lucky run doesn't mean much

  • 01No signal, no score. If we can't see the flicker on both sides, or fewer than 2 sensors have good contact, it just says "Not interpretable."
  • 02Decided before the test. 12 trials, pass at 10, and which sensors we use are all set before it starts. Passing by pure luck is about a 4% chance.
  • 03No retrying until it works. The server allows one scored run per patient every 12 hours. Stopping halfway still counts as a run.
  • 04All runs show up on the report, including failed and stopped ones.
  • 05It never says "conscious" or "unconscious". A failed test doesn't mean nobody is there.

What this is based on

  • Morgan, Hansen & Hillyard 1996: showed that paying attention to one side makes its flicker response stronger. They also used 8.6 and 12 Hz.
  • Lesenfants 2014: covert-attention SSVEP in locked-in syndrome.
  • Pan 2014: visual awareness test in disorders of consciousness.
  • g.tec mindBEAGLE: the clinical system, using hearing, touch and imagined movement.

Limitations

  • It's a hackathon prototype on a non-medical headset (Unicorn BCI Core-4).
  • We only tested it on ourselves. Using it with patients would need a proper approved study.
  • Needs open eyes, a working visual pathway and language.
  • Attention-only accuracy is around 70 to 75% even in healthy people, so a lot of real responders would still get "No conclusion".
  • Flicker is not risk-free for photosensitive people.

One line per sensor. Wavy is good. A flat line means that sensor isn't touching. Big jumps usually mean blinking, jaw clenching or moving.

How scoring works

12 turns. Pass at 10 or more (guessing gets about 6). One pass means "test again later". Two passes in a row means "refer". Bad signal means no score at all.

Advanced: old visual-field experiments (not part of StillHere)

The flicker tests flash at 8.57 Hz and 12 Hz. Flicker from 3 to 60 Hz can trigger seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy; 15 to 20 Hz is the most provocative. It is not risk-free. Do not start if you have photosensitive epilepsy or if flickering light has made you unwell. Stop if you feel discomfort.
